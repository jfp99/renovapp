Read this first. All four units are identical. Quote and build them as one batch — set up the jig once and repeat. Do not quote per unit as if each were a one-off.

Every dimension below is in centimetres. Steel sizes are given in the trade sizes sold in 6.00 m lengths at any Bacolod hardware. No custom sections, no machining, no bought-in hardware except casters. If any item on the bill of materials is unavailable, call before substituting — do not reduce wall thickness.

1 · Side elevation

Fig. 1 — Side elevation. Ladder is at the far end, not visible here (see Fig. 2).

The three heights that matter, and why.

  • 50 cm to the lower deck — this is what creates the space for the rolling trays. A normal bunk sits at 35 cm.
  • 88 cm sitting headroom on the lower berth — enough for an adult to sit upright in bed. Below 80 cm the lower berth becomes unrentable.
  • 30 cm guard rail above the upper mattress. This is a safety minimum, not a preference. Do not reduce it, and do not raise the mattress thickness beyond 15 cm.

2 · End elevation and ladder

Fig. 2 — Foot end.

Ladder — build it into the frame

There is no separate ladder. Five flat bars are welded directly between the two foot-end posts, first rung at 25 cm, then every 30 cm. Nothing to store, nothing to fall over, nothing to steal.

  • Rungs are flat bar 32 × 5 mm laid flat, never round bar. Round bar hurts under a bare foot and students stop using the top bunk.
  • Weld the full length of both ends of every rung. A rung tacked at two points will work loose within a year.
  • Grind every rung edge smooth. This is the part hands and feet touch every day.

Head end

Guard rail on the head end of the upper berth: two horizontal 1" tubes between the posts, same heights as the side rails. The foot end stays open for the ladder.

3 · Room layout and tray clearance

Fig. 3 — Bedroom 1 (320 × 260). Bedroom 2 (330 × 310) is larger: 131 cm aisle, 111 cm foot space. Same units, no change.

The layout is what makes the trays work. The two bunks sit against opposite walls, not side by side. That leaves a 121 cm central aisle, and both bunks pull their trays into it. Side by side, only the outer bunk would have access and half the storage would be dead.

One consequence to accept: if all four trays are pulled fully at the same moment they meet in the middle. In practice these hold luggage and are opened twice a semester — this is not a real problem, but do not plan on more than 60 cm of travel.

4 · Component details

Four sub-assemblies, drawn separately. Each is dimensioned and each is deliberately made from material already on the bill of materials — nothing here requires an extra trip to the hardware.

4.1 · Bedside shelf — 4 per unit, one per berth

Fig. 4 — Bedside shelf. Same bracket for both berths: 15 cm above the deck rail puts the shelf level with the top of the mattress.

Position: at the head end of the aisle side, both berths. Lower shelf top at 65 cm, upper at 170 cm. Reachable lying down, and tucked into the corner of the room where nobody walks.

The bracket is one right triangle of 1" tube — a 22 cm horizontal, a 15 cm vertical welded to the deck rail, and the hypotenuse. Two per shelf, 26 cm apart. Cut all eight from post offcuts. Round the two outer corners of every plate to 3 cm radius: at 170 cm a square corner is at face height for an adult.

The 2 cm lip on three sides is not decorative. Without it, the upper occupant's water glass lands on the lower occupant.

4.2 · Rolling tray — 2 per unit

Fig. 5 — Rolling tray. Open top, 15 cm lip on all four sides, grab bar on the pull side.

The tray is fully removable — there is no travel stop and none is wanted. Pull it out, take the suitcase, push it back; lift it out entirely to clean under the bed. Removing the stop removes a part, a weld and a jamming point.

Floor is marine plywood, not ordinary — it is the one surface that meets a wet mopped floor. Seal all six faces including the cut edges.

Casters: 50 mm swivel, no brake. Brakes get kicked, break, and then the tray will not roll. Fix the casters with bolts through the frame, not welded — a caster is a wear part and must be replaceable with a spanner.

4.3 · Curtain rods — privacy for both berths

Fig. 6 — Curtain rods. Only one part is fabricated: the lower rod.

Upper berth: nothing to fabricate. The top guard rail at 200 cm already is the rod. Curtain rings slide straight onto it. Drop 45 cm.

Lower berth: one Ø12 mm round bar, 191 cm, welded between the two aisle-side posts at 148 cm. Weld it to the outer face of the posts so it stands 4 cm proud of the frame and the rings run freely. Supported at both ends, so it cannot sag. Drop 98 cm.

Curtains and rings are soft furnishing, bought separately — not the welder's scope. Specify flame-retardant fabric: it is cheap, and it is the kind of detail a fire inspector notices in your favour.

4.4 · Electrical provision — welder's scope is clips only

Fig. 7 — Electrical provision. The welder supplies the four clips. Everything else is the electrician's.

Split of scope, and it is not negotiable. The welder welds four Ø20 mm conduit clips to the head-end post and stops there. A licensed electrician runs the conduit, fits the boxes, and connects the circuit. A welder wiring outlets is exactly what gets a Fire Safety Inspection Certificate refused.

For the electrician: one dedicated circuit per bedroom, 2.0 mm² minimum, own 15 A breaker at the panel, 4 outlets plus the ceiling fan. Each outlet a duplex socket with integrated USB, boxed and surface-mounted.

Reading lights are clip-on LED lamps bought retail and plugged in. Not hardwired, not fabricated. They cost ₱250, any occupant can replace one, and nothing has to be rewired when a model is discontinued.

Why one outlet per berth matters more than it looks: four students sharing one socket means a daisy-chain of extension cords across the floor. That is the most common cause of dormitory fires in the Philippines, and it is designed out here for about ₱3,500 a room.

What survives all this stripping-out is the part the occupant actually touches: her own shelf, her own socket, her own curtain, her own tray. That is deliberate. The savings come out of the steel, not out of the four things that make a bed space feel private.

7 · Welding and assembly notes

  1. Full welds, not tack welds, on every load-bearing joint. Tack-welded bunks are the single reason cheap steel beds squeak. This is the most important line in this document.
  2. Square everything before final welding. Check both diagonals of each deck frame; they must match within 3 mm. Tack, check, then weld out.
  3. Grind every weld and every cut edge smooth. No sharp corners anywhere a hand or a mattress can reach.
  4. Cap all open tube ends with welded plate. Open ends collect water and rust from inside.
  5. No tray travel stops. The trays are meant to come out completely. Instead, make sure the tray frame's four corners are ground smooth — they will be handled.
  6. Bolt the casters, do not weld them. They are wear parts and must come off with a spanner.
  7. Round the outer corners of all shelf plates to 3 cm radius before fitting.
  8. Rubber pads under all four posts, bolted or glued, so the frame does not scratch the floor or transmit noise.
  9. Four conduit clips per unit, welded to the head-end post at 40, 90, 140 and 180 cm, sized for Ø20 mm electrical conduit (Fig. 7). Wiring is not in your scope.
  10. Deliver the units knocked down if the stairs require it, but the frames must be welded, not bolted. Confirm access before fabrication.

8 · Finish

  • Degrease, then remove all mill scale and weld spatter with a wire wheel.
  • One coat epoxy primer — not red-lead oxide primer. Humidity here is the enemy.
  • Two coats enamel topcoat, light neutral colour. Confirm colour before painting.
  • Plywood: seal all six faces including the cut edges, two coats. This applies to the deck panels, the four shelf plates and the tray floors and lips. Unsealed plywood edges are where moisture and insects get in.
  • Do not paint over rust, and do not paint the same day you weld — let the metal cool fully.

9 · Acceptance — the tests to be passed before final payment

  1. Climb test. Two adults on the upper berth at once. Any squeak, any flex, any movement in a joint = rejected. Test on site, not in the shop.
  2. Rock test. Push the frame hard at the top of a post. It must not rack.
  3. Tray test. Load a full suitcase, pull and push twenty times, then lift the tray out completely and put it back. Must roll without binding on a bare cement floor.
  4. Shelf test. 5 kg on the outer edge of every shelf, upper and lower. No sag, no movement at the bracket. Then run a hand over the rounded corners.
  5. Curtain test. Rings must slide the full length of both rods without catching on a weld.
  6. Level test. Spirit level on both decks, both directions.
  7. Hand test. Run a bare hand over every rung, edge and weld. Anything that catches gets ground down before payment.
  8. Fit test. Both units assembled in the room with the 121 cm aisle clear and trays pulling freely.
